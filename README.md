# Introduction

## Prenatal maternal smoking and offspring birth weight

Birth weight is an important indicator of health at the beginning of life and a central outcome in research on the conditions that shape fetal development. Low birth weight, defined as a weight at birth below 2,500 grams, remains a substantial global public health concern. An estimated 19.8 million infants, representing 14.7% of live births worldwide, were born with low birth weight in 2020 (Okwaraji et al., 2024). These estimates describe a specific historical year rather than the prevalence at the time of this thesis. Low birth weight can arise from shortened gestation, restricted fetal growth, or a combination of both, making it an informative but heterogeneous marker of prenatal health. Understanding its determinants therefore requires attention to both the biological processes of pregnancy and the social conditions in which pregnancy occurs. [1]

The relevance of birth weight extends beyond the threshold used to classify low birth weight. A continuous measure preserves differences across the distribution, including among infants who would not meet the clinical definition of low birth weight. This is useful when an exposure may shift average birth weight without causing every exposed infant to cross a diagnostic threshold. The present study consequently examines birth weight in ounces as a continuous outcome. Its estimates describe mean differences in birth weight associated with prenatal smoking; they do not directly estimate the probability of low birth weight, neonatal complications, or later developmental impairment.

Maternal cigarette smoking during pregnancy is a particularly important exposure because it is potentially modifiable and has been repeatedly associated with adverse birth outcomes. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 55 cohort studies reported that maternal smoking during pregnancy was associated with higher odds of low birth weight, with a pooled odds ratio of 1.89 and a 95% confidence interval of 1.80–1.98 (Di et al., 2022). This synthesis establishes the consistency of the observational association, although a pooled association does not by itself determine how much of the relationship is attributable to intrauterine exposure and how much reflects differences between mothers who smoke and those who do not. Moreover, an odds ratio for low birth weight and a regression coefficient for continuous birth weight answer different questions and should not be interpreted as interchangeable measures. [2]

There is also a biological basis for concern about prenatal tobacco exposure. Cigarette smoke introduces nicotine, carbon monoxide, and other substances that can interfere with the conditions supporting fetal development. Nicotine-related vascular effects and impaired oxygen delivery associated with carbon monoxide provide plausible pathways through which smoking may influence fetal growth. Clinical guidance identifies fetal growth restriction and low birth weight among the risks associated with tobacco use during pregnancy and emphasizes the benefits of cessation (American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, 2020). The present analysis does not measure these biological pathways directly; they provide a rationale for investigating the exposure rather than mechanisms that can be established from the regression results. [3]

## The problem of confounding in observational research

The central methodological challenge is that maternal smoking is not randomly distributed across pregnancies or families. Smoking behavior may be related to education, economic resources, psychological distress, other substance use, access to care, and enduring characteristics of the maternal environment. Some of these factors may also influence birth weight. An observed difference between infants exposed and unexposed to prenatal smoking can therefore combine an association attributable to smoking with associations attributable to other maternal and family characteristics.

Conventional multivariable regression addresses this problem by adjusting for measured covariates. In this study, the adjusted model includes child sex, race and ethnicity, gestational age, prenatal alcohol use, maternal age, and maternal Armed Forces Qualifications Test (AFQT) score. These variables describe several dimensions along which exposed and unexposed children may differ. However, including them in a regression does not ensure that the two groups are otherwise comparable. Relevant characteristics may be absent, measured imperfectly, or represented by variables that capture only part of a broader social or behavioral process.

The AFQT score illustrates the distinction between an observed measure and the underlying characteristics it is intended to represent. It provides information about maternal performance on a standardized assessment, but it cannot fully summarize educational opportunity, material resources, health knowledge, or the circumstances influencing smoking behavior. Similarly, race and ethnicity should not be interpreted as biological explanations for differences in birth weight. Their inclusion may account for some observed group differences while leaving unmeasured the social exposures and structural conditions associated with those classifications. Interpretation therefore depends on the role of each variable in the research question, rather than on the assumption that a longer adjustment set necessarily removes more bias.

Gestational age requires particular attention because it may be affected by smoking and is closely related to birth weight. If prenatal smoking influences the duration of pregnancy, adjusting for gestational age can block part of the pathway through which smoking affects weight at delivery. The gestational-age-adjusted association may therefore differ from the total association of interest. Such adjustment can also introduce bias under some causal structures. Consequently, the adjusted model in this thesis should be interpreted as a conditional association, not automatically as an estimate of the total causal effect of smoking. Comparing it with models that do not adjust for gestational age requires acknowledging this difference in interpretation (Hernán and Robins, 2020). [4]

These considerations motivate an approach that addresses stable maternal and family characteristics through study design. A comparison between unrelated children relies heavily on measured information about their different families. A comparison between children born to the same mother can remove the influence of characteristics that remain constant across her pregnancies, including characteristics that were never recorded in the dataset.

## The rationale for sibling comparisons

A sibling fixed-effects design uses differences between pregnancies within the same maternal family. When a mother reports smoking during one pregnancy but not another, her children provide a within-family comparison of exposed and unexposed births. Including a fixed effect for each mother accounts for stable maternal characteristics and other family factors that are constant across the observed pregnancies. The smoking coefficient is consequently identified by within-mother variation in exposure rather than by differences in smoking prevalence between mothers.

This design is valuable because many potential confounders have persistent components. Maternal genetic characteristics, aspects of early-life background, and enduring dispositions may influence both smoking behavior and pregnancy outcomes. Mother fixed effects absorb their time-invariant contribution to the modeled outcome. However, this does not mean that siblings have identical genomes or environments. Children can differ genetically, may have different fathers, and can experience different household circumstances. Income, partnership status, residential location, maternal health, and access to care may also change between pregnancies. Fixed effects control only the components that are constant within the maternal family over the observations used.

The design has already been applied to prenatal smoking and birth weight. Juárez and Merlo (2013) compared conventional and sibling estimates using Swedish population data and found that birth-weight reductions associated with smoking were less pronounced in the sibling analysis. The degree of attenuation varied with smoking intensity and the timing of exposure assessment. This study supports examining whether estimates change when stable family characteristics are controlled, while also showing why a single binary exposure measure cannot capture all dimensions of prenatal smoking. The contribution of the present thesis is therefore an application in a distinct United States longitudinal cohort, rather than the introduction of sibling comparisons to this research area. [5]

Sibling comparisons have limitations of their own. Factors that change between pregnancies can remain confounders, and within-family estimates may be especially sensitive to nonshared confounding and exposure measurement error (Frisell et al., 2012). A reported change in smoking status may also be misclassified. Accordingly, a smaller coefficient after adding mother fixed effects is compatible with reduced familial confounding, but does not establish that all attenuation represents confounding removed. A remaining association likewise does not, by itself, prove a biological mechanism or an unconfounded causal effect. [6]

The exposure variation available within families also defines the scope of inference. Families in which smoking status changes across pregnancies supply the within-family smoking variation needed to estimate the coefficient. These mothers may differ from mothers who consistently smoke or consistently abstain. The within-family association therefore need not represent the association that would be observed under a smoking intervention across all pregnancies. This limitation makes it useful to interpret population-level and sibling estimates together while recognizing that they draw on different comparisons.

## Study setting and analytic approach

The National Longitudinal Survey of Youth 1979 Child and Young Adult cohort provides an opportunity to examine these questions in the United States. The child survey began in 1986 and follows children born to women in the original NLSY79 cohort. Its linked maternal and child records include information on prenatal behaviors, gestation, birth weight, and family background. These features permit both comparisons across mothers and comparisons among children born to the same mother. The cohort represents the children of a defined group of women followed over time; an analytic subset should not automatically be treated as representative of every United States birth cohort. [7,8]

The thesis analytic sample comprises 8,744 children. The sibling fixed-effects analysis uses 7,093 children born to 2,775 mothers, of whom 454 report different smoking statuses across their observed pregnancies. These sample figures describe the study documented in the accompanying thesis. Prenatal smoking is operationalized as a binary indicator, and birth weight is measured continuously in ounces. This exposure definition supports a comparison of reported smoking with reported nonsmoking, but does not distinguish daily cigarette consumption, trimester-specific exposure, or the timing of cessation.

Three models organize the investigation. An unadjusted linear regression estimates the overall mean birth-weight difference associated with smoking. A multivariable regression examines the association conditional on the measured child and maternal covariates. A mother fixed-effects model then examines the within-family association while adjusting for child sex and birth year. These models provide complementary descriptions of the data: the first summarizes the crude contrast, the second incorporates measured differences, and the third addresses stable maternal and family characteristics through within-mother comparisons.

The comparison is not a pure decomposition of familial confounding. The fixed-effects analysis uses a smaller sample and a different covariate set from the conventional adjusted model. Changes in coefficients may therefore reflect differences in sample composition, conditioning variables, exposure variation, or remaining sources of bias, as well as control of stable family characteristics. In particular, the fixed-effects model does not include gestational age, whereas the multivariable model does. The magnitude of attenuation should consequently be treated as a descriptive comparison across modeling approaches rather than as an exact percentage of the association explained by family background.

Propensity score matching provides a supplementary comparison based on measured characteristics. In the accompanying analysis, exposed and unexposed observations are matched using the covariates included in the multivariable model. Agreement between matching and regression can help assess sensitivity to these particular approaches to measured-covariate adjustment. It cannot establish that unmeasured confounding has been eliminated, and matching on gestational age retains the interpretive concern associated with conditioning on a potential mediator. The matching and sibling analyses therefore address related but distinct questions.

## Research question and objectives

The primary research question is: **To what extent is maternal smoking during pregnancy associated with offspring birth weight in the NLSY79 Child and Young Adult cohort, and how does the estimated association change when comparisons account for measured characteristics and stable maternal and family factors?**

The first objective is to characterize the unadjusted association between reported prenatal smoking and continuous birth weight. The second is to estimate the association after adjustment for the measured covariates included in the study. The third is to estimate the within-mother association using a sibling fixed-effects model. A supplementary objective is to compare the conventional adjusted result with the propensity-score-matched analysis. Together, these objectives assess whether the direction and magnitude of the association are sensitive to the comparison being made and the characteristics being controlled.

The substantive hypothesis is that prenatal smoking is associated with lower offspring birth weight. A related methodological hypothesis is that the association will be smaller in within-family comparisons if stable familial characteristics contribute to the conventional association. These hypotheses do not presume that attenuation must occur or that a statistically significant within-family coefficient establishes causality. They instead organize an assessment of the evidence across models with different strengths and limitations.

This study contributes to maternal and child health research by examining a well-established risk relationship through several analytic perspectives in the same longitudinal data source. Its value lies in clarifying how the estimate depends on measured adjustment and within-family comparisons, while keeping the interpretation aligned with the available exposure information and study design. Such clarification supports a more precise discussion of prenatal smoking, birth weight, and the family circumstances that accompany both, without extending the findings to biological mechanisms or long-term outcomes that the analysis does not directly measure.
